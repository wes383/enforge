# Measurement Problems in Remote Work Research

## The core problem

Most claims about remote work productivity rest on measures that do not measure output. Across the 27 studies we reviewed, 19 relied on self-report and only 6 used an objective output measure. The remaining two used supervisor ratings, which correlate with visibility rather than output.

## Hours are not output

In 41 percent of the reviewed studies the outcome variable was hours logged or hours connected. Hours are an input. A system that records more connected hours has not shown higher output, and treating the two as interchangeable explains much of the disagreement in the literature.

## Survivorship in firm data

Firm-level studies often compare the same employees before and after a transition. Employees who left during the transition are excluded, which removes the workers most likely to have been hurt by the change. In the one study that retained leavers in the denominator, the measured gain fell from 9 percent to 2 percent.

## What would count as evidence

Adequate evidence needs an objective output measure, a comparison group that did not change arrangements, and retention of leavers in the denominator. Three studies in the review meet all three criteria. Their combined estimate is a 2 percent gain with an interval that crosses zero.

## Implication

Reports of 20 percent gains and reports of 20 percent losses can both be true of their own measurement systems while neither describes productivity. The disagreement is a measurement artefact before it is a disagreement about remote work.
