# A Meta-Analysis of 27 Remote Work Studies Finds No Reliable Productivity Effect

## Scope

This review pooled 27 studies covering 41,000 workers published between 2015 and 2022. The pooled standardised mean difference in productivity was 0.04 with a 95 percent confidence interval from minus 0.03 to 0.11. The effect does not differ significantly from zero.

## Why the literature disagrees

The studies do not agree, and the disagreement is systematic. Sixty-one percent of the pooled studies relied on self-reported productivity. Studies using objective output measures reported a mean effect of 0.07; studies using supervisor ratings reported 0.19. The gap is larger than the pooled estimate itself.

## Trust and retention

Trust scores, measured on a 0 to 100 scale, fell by 7 points on average in the nine studies that tracked them longitudinally. Voluntary attrition fell by 12 percent in the same set. Remote work appears to reduce both the risk of leaving and the strength of reported trust.

## Contradiction with industry surveys

The pooled evidence does not support the large gains reported by manager surveys. One widely cited survey claimed a 20 percent output increase, but that survey sampled managers who had volunteered to describe their own remote programmes. Self-selected respondents overstate gains by an unknown margin.

## Conclusion

The defensible claim is narrow. On current evidence the average effect of remote work on productivity is close to zero, with high variance between settings. Any single study reporting a double-digit gain or loss should be read as describing one setting rather than a general rule.
