# A Systematic Review of Microplastic Toxicology: What the Animal and Human Evidence Supports

## Abstract

We reviewed 40 experimental studies of microplastic exposure, of which 31 were rodent studies and 9 were human cell or biomarker studies. No study demonstrated a consistent adverse effect at concentrations reported in drinking water. The human evidence does not show a dose-response relationship.

## Animal evidence

Rodent studies reporting intestinal inflammation administered doses between 1 and 100 milligrams per kilogram of body weight per day. The lowest dose producing a reproducible effect was 5 milligrams per kilogram. Estimated human intake from drinking water is below 0.01 milligrams per kilogram per day, roughly three orders of magnitude lower. Studies using environmentally relevant doses reported effects in 4 of 19 cases, and two of those four failed to replicate.

## Human evidence

The nine human studies measured biomarkers rather than clinical outcomes. Six found no association after adjustment. Three found small associations with inflammatory markers, with effect sizes below 0.2 standard deviations. None measured a clinical endpoint such as cardiovascular events or kidney function decline.

## Does the evidence contradict the alarm

The consumer reports that describe universal contamination and the toxicology literature that finds no established effect at current exposure levels are not measuring the same thing. Detection is not harm. A particle counted at 3 particles per millilitre is a finding about presence; it is not a finding about risk.

## Conclusion

On current evidence, drinking-water microplastic exposure at reported concentrations has not been shown to cause human disease. The literature does not establish safety either, because the exposure estimates are uncertain and the human studies are short. The honest claim is that the question is open, not that the answer is alarming.
